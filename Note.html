Introduction to Metric Spaces

Metric spaces were introduced by Fréchet in his PhD dissertation in 1906. The mathematicians of that time were studying various spaces (mainly spaces of functions) and they had various notions of convergence in such spaces. For each space its own notion of convergence was introduced, and studied. Of course, similarities were noticed and Fréchet realised that these arguments can be unified by introducing an abstract concept of a metric or distance function. He could show that many of these spaces were instances of his new concept of a metric space. Then, by proving one result axiomatically from the metric axioms, it automatically holds for all instances.

In the modern view, the concept of a metric space is just an axiomatisation of the notion of distance. As we will see, different notions of distance are very important since they occur all the time in mathematics, statistics and physics. Many geometric objects rely on a notion of distance (e.g. the unit sphere in ℝn\mathbb R^n is exactly the set of points at distance 11 from the origin). So, it is natural to distill some common properties of distances in various contexts and set them as axioms.

As already mentioned above, distance plays a fundamental role in the definition of convergence. Let us recall that definition from calculus:

Definition 1. Let (xn)n(x_n)_n be a sequence of real numbers and x∈ℝx\in \mathbb R. Then (xn)n(x_n)_n converges to xx, if for every ε>0\varepsilon>0 there exists an N∈ℕN\in \mathbb{N} such that |x−xn|<ε\vert x-x_n\vert<\varepsilon for all n≥Nn\geq N.

In other words: for every error tolerance (the ε\varepsilon) the distance between xnx_n and xx is eventually (i.e. past the the index NN) less than the error tolerance.

Our first goal is to formalise the concept of distance. This is the content of the following definition:

Definition 2. Let XX be a non-empty set. A metric (or distance function) on XX is a function d:X×X→ℝ,(x,y)↦d(x,y)d \colon X \times X \to \mathbb R, \ (x,y)\mapsto d(x,y) satisfying the following three properties:

  1. d(x,y)=0d(x,y) = 0 if and only if x=yx=y;

  2. d(x,y)=d(y,x)d(x,y) = d(y,x) for all x,y∈Xx,y \in X;

  3. d(x,y)≤d(x,z)+d(z,y)d(x,y) \leq d(x,z) + d(z,y) for all x,y,z∈Xx,y,z \in X.

The pair (X,d)(X,d) is called a metric space.

Let us record a few remarks on the axioms in the definition above:

  • (M1) says that each point has zero distance from itself, and that distinct points must have a non-zero distance.

  • (M2) says that the distance from xx to yy is the same as the distance from yy to xx. We say that dd is symmetric.

  • (M3) is called the triangle inequality and gets its name from the fact that the length of any side of a triangle is at most the sum of the lengths of the other two sides. It says that a journey from xx to yy doesn’t get any shorter if you take a detour via zz, but may possibly get longer.

  • We can combine the axioms to prove that the distance between any two points must be non-negative: indeed, for all x,y∈Xx,y\in X we have 2d(x,y)=d(x,y)+d(x,y)=(M2)d(x,y)+d(y,x)≥(M3)d(x,x)=(M1)0,2d(x,y)=d(x,y)+d(x,y)\stackrel{(M2)}{=}d(x,y)+d(y,x)\stackrel{(M3)}{\geq} d(x,x)\stackrel{(M1)}{=}0, so dividing by 22 gives d(x,y)≥0d(x,y)\geq 0.

Example 6 (Discrete metric). On any set X≠∅X \neq\emptyset the function $$d(x,y) = \begin{cases} 1 & \hbox{ if } x \neq y, \\ 0 & \hbox{ if } x = y \end{cases}$$ defines a metric called the discrete metric. Such ’pathological’ examples, as they are nicknamed, are not normally used in applications in analysis. They serve as a warning to check by rigorous proofs that results suggested by intuition really hold in general metric spaces. In other words, they are potential counterexamples; they explore the boundaries of the concept of a metric space.

Example 7. On the complex plane ℂ\mathbb{C} the French Railway metric is given as $$d_{f}(z_{1},z_{2})=\begin{cases} 0 & \hbox{if } z_{1}=z_{2},\\ |z_{1}|+|z_{2}| & \hbox{if } z_{1}\neq z_{2} \end{cases}$$ is a metric (folklore suggests that the shortest rail journey between any two French towns is via Paris).

Example 8 (Metric Subspaces). If (X,d)(X,d) is a metric space and Y⊆XY\subseteq X is a subset of XX, then we can define the induced metric dYd_Y on YY as the restriction of dd to Y×Y⊆X×XY\times Y\subseteq X\times X, i.e. dY(x,y)=d(x,y)d_Y(x,y)=d(x,y) for all x,y∈Yx,y\in Y.

If these were the only examples of metric spaces it is doubtful whether general metric space theory would be worthwhile. The examples below indicate the wide range of metric space theory (but do not exhaust it).

Example 9. The following metric plays an important role in number theory. Let pp be a fixed prime number. Example 10 (The word metric on a finitely generated group). This example will only make sense if you know about groups and generating sets. Suppose GG is a finitely generated group and SS is a generating set for GG. That is every element g∈Gg\in G can be written as a product g=g1⋯gng=g_1\cdots g_n of elements in SS (and their inverses). Example 11 (Geodesic distance on a graph). Let (V,E)(V,E) be a connected graph (undirected, without multiple edges between vertices), with vertex set VV and edge set EE. Then we can define a metric on VV by letting d(v,w)d(v,w) be the length of the shortest path between the two vertices vv and ww. This metric is important in the study of networks.

Example 12 (Hamming distance). Let Σ={a,b,c,…,z}\Sigma=\{a,b,c,\ldots, z\} be the modern Latin alphabet. Let XX be the set of all strings of letters in Σ\Sigma of length 4 (e.g. abcdabcd is an element in XX, so are mathmath or ioskiosk). The Hamming distance dHd_H between two strings in XX is the number of positions at which the corresponding symbols are different. In other words, it measures the minimum number of substitutions required to change one string into the other. One can check that dHd_H is indeed a metric on XX. A major application of the Hamming distance is in coding theory.

Sequences in Metric Spaces

Now that we have formalised the notion of distance and a lot of examples to play with we can define convergence of sequences in any metric space:

Definition 16. Let (X,d)(X,d) be a metric space and (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} a sequence in XX. We say that (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} converges to xx (shorthand notation xn→xx_n\rightarrow x, or limn→∞xn=x\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} x_n=x) if for every ε>0\varepsilon>0 there exists an N∈ℕN\in\mathbb N such that d(xn,x)<εd(x_n,x)<\varepsilon for all n≥Nn\geq N.

Given a sequence (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} in XX and x∈Xx\in X we can consider the sequence of distances (d(xn,x))n∈ℕ(d(x_n,x))_{n\in \mathbb N} in [0,∞)[0,\infty). Comparing the definition above with the one from the intro it is clear that (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} converges to xx in (X,d)(X,d) if and only if the sequence of distances (d(xn,x))n∈ℕ(d(x_n,x))_{n\in \mathbb N} converges to 00 in the usual sense of convergence of sequences of real numbers!

We want to observe that if a sequence in a metric space converges, then its limit is unique. It might come as a surprise why this requires a proof at all, but we will later (in a more general context) see that this is indeed an important feature of metric spaces.

Lemma 17. Let (X,d)(X,d) be a metric space and (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} be a sequence in XX and x,y∈Xx,y\in X. If xn→xx_n\rightarrow x and xn→yx_n\rightarrow y, then x=yx=y.

Proof. Observe first that the conclusion x=yx=y is equivalent to d(x,y)<εd(x,y) <\varepsilon for all ε>0\varepsilon>0. Thus, it suffices to show the latter. So let ε>0\varepsilon>0 be given. Then there exist N1,N2∈ℕN_1,N_2\in \mathbb N such that d(xn,x)<ε2d(x_n,x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2} for all n≥N1n\geq N_1 and d(xn,y)<ε2d(x_n,y)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2} for all n≥N2n\geq N_2. Let N:=max{N1,N2}N:=\max \{N_1,N_2\}. Then using the triangle-inequality (M3) we have d(x,y)≤d(x,xN)+d(xN,y)<εd(x,y)\leq d(x,x_N)+d(x_N,y)<\varepsilon Since ε\varepsilon was aribtrary it follows that d(x,y)=0d(x,y)=0 and hence x=yx=y by axiom (M1). ◻

Note also that this result justifies the notation limn→∞xn\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} x_n for the limit.

From real analysis we know a lot of tools to check if a sequence in ℝ\mathbb R converges, but checking if a sequence in a given metric space converges seems like a much more daunting task. It is helpful to observe that some metrics have the same convergent sequences. If that is the case, we can just chose the one that seems easier to us to check convergence. Formally we make the following definition:

Definition 18. Two metrics dd and d′d' on a set XX are Lipschitz equivalent if there are constants C,D>0C,D>0 such that $$d(x,y)\leq Cd'(x,y)\ \hbox{ and }\ d'(x,y) \leq D d(x,y)$$ for all x,y∈Xx,y\in X.

Cauchy sequences

Here is another useful concept that can be carried over from real analysis without any trouble.

Definition 23. Let (X,d)(X,d) be a metric space. A sequence (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} in XX is called a Cauchy sequence if for all ε>0\varepsilon>0 there exists an N∈ℕN\in \mathbb N such that d(xn,xm)<εd(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon for all n,m≥Nn,m\geq N.

The proofs of the following two elementary facts about Cauchy sequences are also almost exactly the same as in calculus:

Lemma 24. Let (X,d)(X,d) be a metric space.

  1. Every convergent sequence in XX is a Cauchy sequence.

  2. Every Cauchy sequence in XX is bounded.

Proof.

  1. Let (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} be a convergent sequence with limit x∈Xx\in X. Let furthermore ε>0\varepsilon >0 be given. Since (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} converges to xx, there exists an N∈ℕN\in \mathbb N such that d(xn,x)<ε/2d(x_n,x)<\varepsilon/2 for all n≥Nn\geq N. Using the triangle-inequality (M3) it follows that for all n,m≥Nn,m\geq N we have d(xn,xm)≤d(xn,x)+d(x,xm)<ε/2+ε/2=ε.d(x_n,x_m)\leq d(x_n,x)+d(x,x_m)<\varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 =\varepsilon.

  2. Let (xn)n∈ℕ(x_n)_{n\in \mathbb N} be a Cauchy sequence. We need to show that there exists a number D≥0D\geq 0 such that d(xn,xm)≤Dd(x_n,x_m)\leq D for all n,m∈ℕn,m\in \mathbb N. The metric topology

We have already seen that the notions of convergence and continuity in the setting of metric spaces do only depend on the metric up to a certain extent. For example, if we replace dd by a Lipschitz-equivalent metric d′d', then sequences converge with respect to dd if and only if they converge with respect to d′d'. In this section we will introduce the concepts on which convergence actually depends. This will eventually lead us the definition of a topological space.

Definition 38. Let (X,d)(X,d) be a metric space, x∈Xx\in X and r>0r>0.

  1. The open ball of radius rr centered at xx is the set Ur(x):={y∈X∣d(x,y)<r},U_r(x):=\{y\in X\mid d(x,y)<r\}, and similarly, the closed ball of radius rr centered at xx is the set Br(x):={y∈X∣d(x,y)≤r}.B_r(x):=\{y\in X\mid d(x,y)\leq r\}.

  2. U⊆XU\subseteq X is called open if for every y∈Uy\in U there exists an r>0r>0 such that Ur(y)⊆UU_r(y)\subseteq U.

  3. A⊆XA\subseteq X is called closed if X\AX\setminus A is open.

Remark 39. We use the word “ball” in a metric space, though these sets may not look very ball-like (depending on the chosen metric). See the exercise sheet for some examples. So the word “ball” is an abuse of terminology.

Warning: A common mistake is to say that if a set is not open then it is closed (or vice versa). THIS IS NOT TRUE!

In metric spaces a set can be:

  1. open and closed, e.g., every point in XX with the discrete metric;

  2. open but not closed, e.g., an open interval (a,b)(a, b) in ℝ\mathbb R with the Euclidean metric;

  3. closed but not open, e.g., a closed interval [a,b][a, b] in ℝ\mathbb R with the Euclidean metric; or

  4. neither open nor closed, e.g., an interval (a,b](a, b] in ℝ\mathbb R with the Euclidean metric.

In “most” spaces, “most” sets are neither open nor closed.

Connected Spaces

In this section we want to study two important notions of connectedness of a space. It turns out that if XX and YY are homeomorphic metric spaces then XX is (path-)connected, if and only if is YY (path-)connected. This a basic instance of a topological invariant which can help us tell metric spaces apart in the following way: if we are given two spaces XX and YY and we can show that XX is (path)-connected and YY is not, then they cannot be homeomorphic to each other.

Definition 60. Let (X,d)(X,d) be a metric space.

  1. A subset A⊆XA\subseteq X is called connected, if whenever UU and VV are open subsets of XX such that A⊆U∪VA\subseteq U\cup V and U∩V∩A=∅U\cap V\cap A=\emptyset, then U∩A=∅U\cap A=\emptyset or V∩A=∅V\cap A=\emptyset.

Finite sets have a number of nice properties, that distinguish them from infinite sets. For example, when XX is a finite set, then

  • every function f:X→ℝf:X\rightarrow \mathbb R is bounded, meaning that there exists a constant M>0M>0 such that |f(x)|≤M\vert f(x)\vert\leq M for all x∈Xx\in X;

  • every function f:X→ℝf:X\rightarrow \mathbb R attains its maximum, meaning that there exists an x0∈Xx_0\in X such that f(x0)≥f(x)f(x_0)\geq f(x) for all x∈Xx\in X;

  • every sequence (xn)n(x_n)_n in XX has a constant sub-sequence.

All of these properties are not terribly hard to prove when XX is finite, but they will become false, when XX is an infinite set like the natural numbers ℕ\mathbb N.

Now we want to turn to metric spaces. Most interesting metric spaces are infinite as sets, but some of them still exhibit properties similar to those of finite sets. From calculus you may remember that a continuous function f:[0,1]→ℝf:[0,1]\rightarrow \mathbb R is bounded and attains its maximum. So despite [0,1][0,1] being infinite, once we require our function to be continuous, it does somehow behave very similar to a function defined on a finite set. There is also an analogue of the third item in the list above: The Bolzano-Weierstraß theorem says that every sequence in [0,1][0,1] has a convergent sub-sequence.

We will see that the reason for all this is that [0,1][0,1] is an example of a compact space and hence ‘small’ in some sense. The rough idea is that spaces which are ‘infinitely large’ such as ℝ\mathbb R or [0,∞)[0,\infty) are not compact. However, we want compactness to depend just on the open sets of the space, so it will have to be defined purely in terms of open sets. This means that any space homeomorphic to a non-compact space will also be non-compact. In particular compactness does not say that the rigid size (like the length, or area) will be small. For example the intervals (0,1)(0, 1) and [0,1)[0, 1) will also be non-compact (despite being subsets of the compact set [0,1][0,1]), since they are homeomorphic to ℝ\mathbb R and [0,∞)[0,\infty) respectively.

So how can this idea be expressed just in terms of open sets rather than in some numerical measure of size? This would seem to be difficult since open sets themselves can be large or small. But large open sets can be expressed as unions of small open sets, so the idea is to count how many small open sets are needed when a large open set in a space X, such as the whole space XX itself, is expressed as a union of small open sets. The most basic question in this situation is whether the number of small open sets needed is finite or infinite. For example, if XX is a metric space, then XX is the union of all its balls Ur(x)U_r(x) of fixed radius r>0r>0, so we could ask whether XX is in fact the union of a finite collection of these balls Ur(x)U_r(x) of fixed radius. This is still not quite general enough: we need to replace balls by arbitrary open sets, and this leads to the following general definition:

Definition 73. Let (X,d)(X,d) be a metric space and K⊆XK\subseteq X a subset.

  1. An open cover of KK in XX is a collection of open subsets 𝒰\mathcal{U} such that K⊆⋃U∈𝒰UK\subseteq \bigcup_{U\in \mathcal{U}} U;

  2. if 𝒱⊆𝒰\mathcal{V}\subseteq \mathcal{U} such that 𝒱\mathcal{V} is still a cover of KK, then we call 𝒱\mathcal{V} a subcover of 𝒰\mathcal{U};

  3. we say that KK is compact if every open cover 𝒰\mathcal{U} of KK admits a finite subcover, i.e. there exists a finite subset 𝒱⊆𝒰\mathcal{V}\subseteq \mathcal{U} such that K⊆⋃U∈𝒱UK\subseteq \bigcup_{U\in \mathcal{V}} U.

The Bolzano-Weierstraß Theorem

  1. (X,d)(X,d) is compact;

  2. Every sequence in (X,d)(X,d) has a convergent subsequence.

Topological Spaces

We have seen that metric spaces allow us to talk about convergence of sequences, the most fundamental notion of analysis. Curiously, in week 44 we were able to reformulate this into a condition that does no longer explicitly depends on the actual metric, but only on the collection of open sets that the metric defines. Similarly, in section 5 we observed that continuity of functions between metric spaces can be equivalently formulated purely in terms of the open subsets of the domain and range of the function. The concepts of being connected (week 6) and compact (weeks 7 and 8) were even just defined in terms of open sets and made no explicit reference to the underlying metric anymore. The idea of topology is to build on this observation and make a paradigm shift, by declaring the collection of open sets to be the central object of interest.

Definition 87. A topology on a set XX is a collection τ\tau of subsets of XX satisfying the following conditions:

  1. ∅∈τ\emptyset\in \tau and X∈τX\in \tau;

  2. τ\tau is closed under arbitrary unions: whenever II is an index set and Ui∈τU_i \in \tau for all i∈Ii \in I then also ⋃i∈IUi∈τ\bigcup_{i \in I} U_i \in \tau;

  3. τ\tau is closed under finite intersections: whenever U1,…,Un∈τU_1, \dots, U_n \in \tau then also ⋂i=1nUi∈τ\bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i \in \tau.

We write (X,τ)(X,\tau) for a topological space, or simply XX if τ\tau is understood. The sets in the family τ\tau are called the open sets of XX.

The notions of the closure, interior and a boundary that we know from the setting of metric spaces also extend to the realm of general topological spaces. Although the following formal definition looks a bit different at first sight:

Definition 96. Let (X,τ)(X,\tau) be a topological space and E⊆XE\subseteq X a subset. Then

Bases in topological spaces and the Hausdorff property

Bases in topological spaces

In a metric space, there is a canonical collection of open sets, namely the open balls of some radius, centered at some point. Now this concept no longer makes sense in an arbitrary topological space. The following concepts are a kind of replacement for them:

Definition 99. Let (X,τ)(X,\tau) be a topological space and x∈Xx\in X.

  1. A neighbourhood (n’hood) of xx is a set A⊆XA\subseteq X such that there exists an open set U⊆XU\subseteq X with x∈U⊆Ax\in U\subseteq A.

  2. A neighbourhood base 𝒰x\mathcal U_x about xx is a collection of n’hoods of xx with the property that for every n’hood VV of xx there exists an element U∈𝒰xU\in \mathcal U_x such that U⊆VU\subseteq V.

  3. We say that (X,τ)(X,\tau) is first-countable if every point x∈Xx\in X admits a countable n’hood base 𝒰x={Un∣n∈ℕ}\mathcal{U}_x=\{U_n\mid n\in \mathbb N\}.

Note that n’hoods (and so elements of n’hood bases) need not be open. In case they are, we will specifically say so.

Examples 100. Let X=ℝX = \mathbb R with the usual topology. An example of a n’hood base for x∈ℝx\in \mathbb R is 𝒰x={[x−ε,x+ε]∣ε∈(0,∞)}.\mathcal{U}_x=\{[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]\mid \varepsilon\in (0,\infty)\}. However, we can find smaller ones as well: For each x∈ℝx\in \mathbb R consider 𝒲x={[x−1n,x+1n]∣n∈ℕ}.\mathcal{W}_x=\{[x-\frac{1}{n},x+\frac{1}{n}]\mid n\in\mathbb N\}. Convergence of sequences proved useful in a metric space to check whether a given set is closed. We do have an analogue of this for certain topological spaces, which we leave as an exercise.

  2. whenever (xn)n(x_n)_n is a sequence in AA such that xn→xx_n\rightarrow x for some x∈Xx\in X, then x∈Ax\in A.

Uniqueness of limits and Hausdorff spaces

The following example shows that limits of sequences in a topological space may no longer be unique.

Example 113. Consider X={a,b,c}X=\{a,b,c\} and τ={∅,X,{a,b}}\tau=\{ \emptyset, X, \{a,b\}\}. It is routine to check that τ\tau is a topology on XX. Define the sequence $$x_n = \begin{cases} a & n \hbox{ is even},\\ b & n \hbox{ is odd}. \end{cases}$$ Then we have

  1. xn→ax_n\rightarrow a because whenever A⊆XA\subseteq X is a neighbourhood of aa, then it must contain {a,b}\{a,b\}. But then xn∈{a,b}⊆Ax_n\in \{a,b\}\subseteq A for all n∈ℕn\in \mathbb N, and

  2. xn→bx_n\rightarrow b because whenever B⊆XB\subseteq X is a neighbourhood of bb, then it must contain {a,b}\{a,b\}. But then xn∈{a,b}⊆Bx_n\in \{a,b\}\subseteq B for all n∈ℕn\in \mathbb N.

The problem in this minimal example is that there are not enough open sets to distinguish the points aa and bb, i.e. we cannot find any neighbourhood of aa that does not also contain bb and vice versa. Now that we have isolated the problem, the following definition will hopefully seem reasonable:

Definition 114. Let (X,τ)(X,\tau) be a topological space. Then (X,τ)(X,\tau) is called a Hausdorff space (also T2T_2 space) if for any two distinct points x,y∈Xx,y\in X there exist open sets U,V∈τU,V\in \tau such that x∈Ux\in U, y∈Vy\in V, and U∩V=∅U\cap V=\emptyset.

The definition says that in a Hausdorff space any two distinct points can be separated by (open) neighbourhoods. It means that the topology contains enough information about XX to distinguish between distinct elements.

Examples 115.

  1. Let XX be a set with at least two points. Then the trivial topology τ={∅,X}\tau=\{\emptyset, X\} is not a Hausdorff topology.

  2. Let XX be any set equipped with the discrete topology τ=𝒫(X)\tau=\mathcal{P}(X). Then (X,τ)(X,\tau) is a Hausdorff space since the singleton set {x}\{x\} is itself a neighbourhood of xx.

  3. Let (X,d)(X,d) be a metric space. Then (X,τd)(X,\tau_d) is a Hausdorff space. Indeed, if x≠yx\neq y then d(x,y)>0d(x,y)>0. So if we set ε:=d(x,y)/2\varepsilon:=d(x,y)/2, then Uε(x)∩Uε(y)=∅U_\varepsilon (x)\cap U_{\varepsilon}(y)=\emptyset.

  4. The space ℱ(ℝ)\mathcal{F}(\mathbb R) with the topology of pointwise convergence is a Hausdorff space. If f≠gf\neq g, then there exists a point x∈ℝx\in \mathbb R such that f(x)≠g(x)f(x)\neq g(x). Let ε:=|f(x)−g(x)|2\varepsilon:=\frac{\vert f(x)-g(x)\vert}{2}. Then Uε(f;x)∩Uε(g;x)=∅.U_\varepsilon(f;x)\cap U_{\varepsilon}(g;x)=\emptyset.

Theorem 116. Let XX be a first-countable topological space. Then the following are equivalent:

  1. XX is a Hausdorff space;

  2. every convergent sequence in XX has a unique limit.
